Mission: Using the book's context and the novel's analysis notes below, write the script for a gripping narrative podcast episode that brings the story to life for the listener.

Writing guidelines:

    Format: Plain text only, with zero formatting — no **bold**, no headings or "###", no bullet or numbered lists, no dashes or "---" separators, no layout symbols of any kind. A full single-voice monologue, ready to be sent as-is straight into text-to-speech: every character will be read aloud, so write only what should be heard. Target length: 1200–1800 words — no more, don't pad it out to hit a length.

    Tone: Immersive, breathless, emotionally charged. Play with pacing and implied silences.

    Intonation & pacing: Write for the ear, not the eye. Punctuation is your only formatting tool — it directly drives the text-to-speech engine's intonation: short, clipped sentences for tension or urgency, commas and ellipses for implied silences, exclamation or question marks for raw emotion. Vary sentence length to build a real narrative breath.

    Storytelling & structure:

        Hook: Drop the listener directly into the book's most intense moment, or into the main character's psychology.

        Throughline: Tell the story like a gripping adventure. Don't just summarize the plot — dissect the characters' choices, the rising tension, and the twists. Use the striking quotes from the analysis notes rather than paraphrasing.

        Immersive analysis: Regularly step back to explain to the listener why a scene works, the hidden symbolism, or what makes this story so memorable — including one honest limitation or reservation about the book, not just praise.

        Conclusion: Leave the listener on a strong note — a reflection on the book's central theme or the memory of a standout scene.

    Strictly avoid: LLM tics ("let's dive into...", "it's important to note that...", "without further ado..."). Write like a storyteller speaking, not like an encyclopedia entry. Never mention "documents", "analysis notes", or sources that were "provided" or "given" to you (e.g. never write "in the documents you provided" or "according to the analysis notes") — you know this novel and tell it directly, as if you'd read it yourself.
